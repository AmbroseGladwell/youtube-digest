## In one line
A finance YouTuber walking through eight minimalist money rules, from cost-per-use to defining your enough, framed by two friends on very different incomes.

## Core claim
Financial stability is a function of the gap between what you earn and what you spend, not of income, so the leverage is in a handful of rules that stop consumption expanding to swallow every raise.

## Key points
- Everything you buy costs three times: the sticker price, the maintenance (storage you pay rent for, insurance, repair, cleaning), and the opportunity cost of not having invested that money.
- Cost per use is the honest price. A good coat at £600 worn a hundred times is £6 a wear; a £60 impulse jacket worn twice is £30 a wear. Cheap can be expensive.
- Parkinson's law applies to money and possessions, not just time: stuff expands to fill the space you have and spending expands to consume the money sitting available. One in, one out forces the trade to be conscious.
- Stop relying on willpower and change the environment instead - take the shopping apps off the phone so you have to open a laptop, unsubscribe from marketing email, don't store the card, and automate the transfer to investments so saving happens without a decision.
- Define your "enough" as a specific monthly number before the money arrives. Without a finish line the goalposts move with every raise, and someone on £200k spending £195k has less freedom than someone on £80k spending £40k.

## Verdict
RECYCLED, but competently. Cost per use, one in one out, automate the saving, define your enough - every one of these has been standard personal-finance content for a decade, and the two-friends framing is the usual device. The three-costs breakdown is the one piece worth keeping. Note also that the numbers (£20k lifetime on phones, £200k across categories) are illustrative arithmetic rather than sourced figures, and the tax-advantaged account she names is a US 401k.

## Try this
- Spend one Saturday afternoon on the replacement test in a single room: for each thing, would you actually spend the money to replace it if it were gone? What fails, goes.
- Remove the stored card details from your phone and take one shopping app off it, keeping it on the desktop only. That single point of friction is the highest-yield change on the list.
- Write down a specific monthly number for your own "enough" - what the life you actually want costs per month - rather than carrying a vague sense of "more".
